WHY WE ARE BUILDING / 01

Keep the context.
Keep the identifiers local.

Why Bowden-PII exists, how the first runtime works, and what a trained baseline does—and does not—mean.

18 September 2026Bowden-PIIDeveloper preview 0.0.1

An email address is useful when sending a reply. An account number is useful when making a payment. Neither necessarily belongs in the text sent to an AI service to summarise a customer request. Yet useful context and personal identifiers often travel together.

Bowden-PII starts at that boundary. Its job is to detect supported identifiers locally, replace them with consistent placeholders, and let an application decide what should leave its environment.

Why Bowden?

Caspar Bowden argued for privacy with close attention to the technical and legal realities of cloud computing. He co-founded the Foundation for Information Policy Research and served as Microsoft's chief privacy adviser for Europe, the Middle East and Africa. EDRi's remembrance describes that work and his independence.

The project takes inspiration from that insistence on understanding where information goes and who can access it. Redacting supported identifiers before transmission is one practical step towards greater control. It does not solve every privacy problem. This is an independent tribute, without affiliation or endorsement from his estate.

Rules first, where rules are useful

The 0.0.1 runtime begins with structured identifiers: Swiss AHV/AVS, UID/CHE, VAT suffixes, IBAN and QR-IBAN, payment-card numbers, email addresses, conservative Swiss phone patterns, URLs, IP addresses and MAC addresses. Where a format has a checksum, the engine validates it rather than relying only on the shape of a string. 

from bowden_pii import redact

result = redact("Contact mia@example.ch", policy="strict")
print(result.redacted)
# Contact [EMAIL_1]

Repeated normalized values reuse a placeholder within the same result. A separate local map retains the original values, so an application can manage them inside its own boundary. The audit summary omits raw detected values. The map—and the CLI's full --json output—still contains originals and needs to stay local.

Three policies control the supported scope. Strict includes all deterministic classes. Balanced preserves URLs. Permissive also preserves IP addresses, MAC addresses and standalone UID values. These choices change what is redacted; they are not compliance settings.

What has actually been trained?

Names, address components and damaged OCR text are harder to capture with format rules. The research track therefore adds a small MiniLM token classifier and combines its predictions with the deterministic engine. Validated rule detections take priority when spans overlap.

Several smoke-training iterations explored negative examples, name boundaries and noisy identifiers. A larger baseline, minilm-large-v1, completed three epochs on 12,000 synthetic training rows. The data strategy includes German, French, Italian and English examples, with separate generated evaluation splits and a curated 120-row stress set.

The recorded evaluations met the project's internal acceptance gate. That is evidence about these datasets, not proof of general reliability. In particular, the hybrid report compares entity label counts; matching counts does not guarantee that every redaction boundary is correct. Broad, independently labelled real-document evaluation remains work to do.

Training progress is not a model release.

Version 0.0.1 includes the rules runtime and experimental hybrid interfaces. It does not ship trained weights or training datasets. The current MiniLM adapter also truncates at 256 tokens by default; it does not yet chunk long documents.

What you can try today

Download the public source or wheel, install it with Python 3.11 or newer, and redact your own text locally through the API or command line. The default rules engine needs no model, runtime dependencies or network connection. The website examples are fixed outputs from that engine; they do not process text entered by visitors.

The public preview has 36 focused tests covering the shipped runtime and its small synthetic benchmark. Those checks support reproducibility, while broader document coverage and real-world evaluation remain essential next steps.

What comes next

The next priorities are better public-document and OCR evaluation, broader identifier coverage, long-text handling, and a clearer path for distributing an evaluated neural checkpoint. Feedback on concrete misses and false positives will be more useful than treating an early benchmark score as a finish line.

Start with synthetic examples, inspect the output, and use the project in the scope it supports today. If you report an issue, provide a minimal synthetic reproduction instead of personal or confidential data.
